Social Engineering Awareness Week

Learn to recognize psychological manipulation tactics used by attackers to trick people into revealing confidential information or performing actions that compromise security.

What is Social Engineering?

🧠

Psychological Manipulation

Social engineering exploits human psychology rather than technical vulnerabilities. Attackers use deception, persuasion, and manipulation to trick victims into divulging sensitive information or granting unauthorized access.

🎯

Common Tactics

Attackers use urgency, authority, fear, curiosity, and trust to manipulate victims. They impersonate trusted figures, create false scenarios, and exploit emotional vulnerabilities to achieve their goals.

⚠️

Real-World Impact

Social engineering attacks are responsible for major data breaches, financial fraud, and corporate espionage. They target employees at all levels and can bypass even the strongest technical security measures.

🛡️

Defense Strategies

Verify identities through independent channels, question unusual requests, follow security protocols, report suspicious contacts, and maintain healthy skepticism of unexpected communications.

Types of Social Engineering Attacks

📞

Pretexting

Attackers create fabricated scenarios (pretexts) to gain trust and extract information. They might impersonate IT support, vendors, or authorities to convince victims to share credentials, financial data, or other sensitive information.

💾

Baiting

Attackers leave physical media (USB drives, CDs) in public places or offer free downloads online. When victims use these items out of curiosity, malware infects their systems, giving attackers access to sensitive data.

🚪

Tailgating / Piggybacking

Unauthorized individuals gain physical access to restricted areas by following authorized personnel. They exploit politeness and social norms, often carrying items or appearing distressed to encourage people to hold doors open.

🎁

Quid Pro Quo

Attackers offer services or benefits in exchange for information or access. Common examples include fake tech support offering to fix computer problems, or surveys offering prizes in return for personal information.

👤

Impersonation

Attackers pose as trusted individuals such as executives, IT staff, or vendors. They use this assumed identity to request sensitive information, authorize transactions, or gain access to secure systems and facilities.

👔

Authority Exploitation

Attackers leverage perceived authority to bypass security protocols. They might claim to be auditors, regulators, or senior executives demanding immediate compliance, exploiting people's tendency to obey authority figures.
